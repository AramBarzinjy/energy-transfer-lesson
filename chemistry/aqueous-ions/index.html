Section A · about 5 minutes

What is a complex ion?

The picture first

Extra help

Put a metal salt in water and the metal ion doesn't float around alone. Six water molecules crowd round it and attach to it. The metal ion plus its six waters is one unit, called a complex ion.

Each water attaches by giving the metal a pair of its own electrons. That's the key idea of the whole section.

Explore [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ in 3D

Class notes · p3

Tip: tap one of the small gold arrows, then turn the model to see that there are six.

The four definitions

Class notes · p3–4
Complex ion
A central atom or ion surrounded by ligands.
Ligand
An ion or molecule with a lone pair of electrons which can form a coordinate bond with a metal ion.
Coordinate bond
A shared pair of electrons which have both come from the same atom.
Coordination number
The number of coordinate bonds a metal ion forms.

The notes say "transition metal ion" in the last one, but it works the same for Al³⁺, which isn't a transition metal.

Trick · read the name

Hexa = 6, aqua = water. So a hexa aqua complex ion is a metal ion with 6 water ligands, coordination number 6, octahedral shape.

Meet the four ions

Only four to learn

Class notes · p4–5

The specification uses just Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺ and Al³⁺. In this topic they always start as hexa aqua ions with an octahedral shape:

Memory trick

Copper is Cool blue. Iron(II) is green like new leaves; iron(III) is brown like rust. Al is always clear.

Why is Fe³⁺ "purple" in the table but yellow-brown in the tube?Pure [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is pale purple. But some of it always loses an H⁺ and becomes [Fe(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺, which is yellow-brown, so the solution looks yellow-brown. You'll see why it loses H⁺ in Section C.

Section C · about 5 minutes

Why 3+ ions are more acidic

The idea in one picture

Extra help

Scroll the diagram sideways to see all of it.

The chain of reasoning

Class notes · p6, handwritten
  1. 3+ ions have a higher charge and are smaller than 2+ ions. So they have a higher charge/size ratio (charge density).
  2. This makes them more polarising: they pull electron density towards themselves more strongly.
  3. The metal pulls electrons out of the water ligand's O, which pulls them out of the O–H bonds. This weakens the O–H bonds.
  4. So H⁺ is more easily released. More H⁺ in solution means the solution is more acidic.
Exam trick · four links, four words

Charge/size → polarising → weakens O–H → releases H⁺. Say all four links and you've got the marks.

Writing it as an equation

Class notes · p6
[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ ⇌ [Fe(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H⁺
[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H⁺

It's just like an ordinary acid: HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. One water ligand loses an H⁺ and becomes an OH⁻ ligand, so the overall charge drops by 1.

The notes use a one-way arrow. It's really an equilibrium (⇌), because only a little dissociates. Either is usually accepted.

Rule to rememberThe M³⁺ aqua ion is always more acidic than the M²⁺ aqua ion.

Explain, in terms of charge/size ratio, why a solution of [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is more acidic than a solution of [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺.

  1. Al³⁺ has a higher charge and is smaller, so it has a higher charge/size ratio and is more polarising. ✔
  2. It weakens the O–H bonds in the water ligands more. ✔
  3. So H⁺ ions are released more easily: [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H⁺. ✔

Section D · about 6 minutes · the most important section

The big pattern: bases pull off H⁺

Section C, taken further

Extra help

In Section C, a water ligand lost one H⁺ by itself. Add a base (OH⁻ or NH₃) and it greedily takes more H⁺ off the water ligands, one after another.

Each H⁺ taken turns an H₂O ligand into an OH⁻ ligand and drops the charge by 1. When the charge reaches zero, the complex is neutral, can't stay dissolved, and comes out as a precipitate.

Watch it happen

Extra help · 3D

Try Cu²⁺ (stops after 2) and then Fe³⁺ (stops after 3).

The rule that writes the formulas for you

Extra help
Trick · charge = number of OH

A 2+ ion loses 2 H⁺ → 2 OH. A 3+ ion loses 3 H⁺ → 3 OH. Waters + OHs always add up to 6.

Two bases, same job

Class notes · p7–8
With NaOH (OH⁻)
OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O, so the by-product is water.
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → [Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2H₂O
With a little NH₃
NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺, so the by-product is ammonium.
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2NH₃ → [Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2NH₄⁺

Same precipitate, same colour, whichever base you use. Only the by-product changes.

Correction to the notes (p7)The handwritten NH₃ equation starts "[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻". With ammonia the base is 2NH₃, which is why 2NH₄⁺ appears on the right.
Iron(II) extraThe green precipitate turns brown on standing in air: oxygen oxidises Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺, so [Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] becomes [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃].
Balancing trick

The number in front of OH⁻ (or NH₃), H₂O (or NH₄⁺) and the number of OH in the product are all the same: the ion's charge. 2+ → all 2s. 3+ → all 3s.

Section E · about 6 minutes

The three exceptions

Almost everything just stops at the precipitate

Extra help

Add excess base and, for nearly every combination, there's no further change. Only three things are different, and that's all you need to remember:

Memory trick

Copper loves ammonia. Aluminium loves hydroxide. Chloride is too fat for six.

See all three in 3D

Extra help · 3D

1 · Copper + excess ammonia

Class notes · p8

The NH₃ molecules replace four water ligands (ligand substitution). The precipitate dissolves and you get a deep blue solution.

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(H₂O)₂(NH₃)₄]²⁺ + 4H₂O

Because the excess is usually added after the blue precipitate has formed, it's often written from the precipitate:

[Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 4NH₃ → [Cu(H₂O)₂(NH₃)₄]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ + 2H₂O
Correction to the notes (p8)The handwritten version writes the precipitate as [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂]²⁺ and leaves the charge off the product. The precipitate is neutral (that's why it's a precipitate), and the deep blue ion is [Cu(H₂O)₂(NH₃)₄]²⁺. Check: left side charge 0; right side 2+ and 2(−1) = 0 ✓.

2 · Aluminium + excess NaOH: amphoteric

AQA colour sheet · spec

Amphoteric means it can react with both acids and bases. Aluminium hydroxide dissolves in both:

as an acid (with base): [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + OH⁻ → [Al(OH)₄]⁻ + 3H₂O
as a base (with acid): [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3H⁺ → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺
Why only with NaOH, not NH₃?Ammonia is a weak base, too weak to pull more H⁺ off. So with excess NH₃ the white precipitate stays: no further change.

3 · Copper + concentrated HCl

Class notes · p9

Chloride ions are significantly larger than water, so fewer fit around the copper ion.

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O
  • Coordination number changes 6 → 4.
  • Shape changes octahedral → tetrahedral.
  • Charge: 2+ and 4 × (−1) = 2−.
  • The mixture may look green, because leftover blue mixes with the yellow [CuCl₄]²⁻.

Section F · about 4 minutes

Carbonate: 2+ and 3+ behave differently

The split

Class notes · p7–8 · AQA sheet
2+ ions (Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺)
Form the metal carbonate precipitate. No fizzing.
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → FeCO₃ + 6H₂O
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CuCO₃ + 6H₂O
3+ ions (Fe³⁺, Al³⁺)
Form the hydroxide precipitate and fizz (CO₂ gas).
2[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3CO₃²⁻ → 2[Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3CO₂ + 3H₂O
2[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3CO₃²⁻ → 2[Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3CO₂ + 3H₂O

The 2+ equations are from the notes. The 3+ equations come from the AQA sheet ("ppt and CO₂ gas evolved").

Why? It's Section C again

Extra help
  1. 3+ aqua ions are acidic enough (they release lots of H⁺) to react with carbonate like an acid does: acid + carbonate → CO₂ + water. That's the fizz.
  2. Losing H⁺ leaves the neutral hydroxide, which precipitates (Pattern 1 again).
  3. 2+ aqua ions are not acidic enough to release CO₂. The carbonate ions just swap in for all the waters and the metal carbonate precipitates.
Memory trick

Three fizzes, two doesn't. 3+ → hydroxide + CO₂. 2+ → MCO₃.

Balancing the 3+ equation

Each 3+ ion loses 3 H⁺; each CO₃²⁻ takes 2 H⁺. Lowest common number is 6, so use 2 metal ions and 3 carbonates → 3CO₂ + 3H₂O.

Explain why CO₂ is given off when sodium carbonate is added to a solution of Al³⁺ ions, but not to a solution of Fe²⁺ ions.

  1. [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is more acidic (higher charge/size ratio, so it releases H⁺ more easily). ✔

The rules

  • Complex ion = metal ion + ligands joined by coordinate bonds (both electrons from the ligand). Hexa aqua: 6 waters, octahedral.
  • 3+ more acidic than 2+: higher charge/size → more polarising → weakens O–H → releases H⁺.
  • Pattern 1: OH⁻ or NH₃ removes H⁺ until neutral → precipitate. 2+ → M(H₂O)₄(OH)₂; 3+ → M(H₂O)₃(OH)₃.
  • Exceptions: Cu + excess NH₃ → deep blue [Cu(H₂O)₂(NH₃)₄]²⁺. Al + excess NaOH → colourless [Al(OH)₄]⁻ (amphoteric). Cu + conc. HCl → yellow [CuCl₄]²⁻, tetrahedral.
  • Carbonate: 2+ → MCO₃ ppt; 3+ → hydroxide ppt + CO₂ fizz.
