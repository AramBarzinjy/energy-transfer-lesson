Section A · about 5 minutes

From sunlight to plant biomass

The big idea

Class notes

A trophic level is an organism's position in the food chain (or food web): producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer and so on.

Energy is lost at each trophic level. Energy is never destroyed. It leaves the food chain, mostly as heat, so less is passed on at each step.

Extra help · link to what you knowPhotosynthesis turns light energy into chemical energy stored in organic molecules: glucose, cellulose, starch, amino acids, DNA and so on. That stored chemical energy is what gets passed along the food chain.

Only about 1–3% of sunlight is captured

Class notes

Only about 1–3% of the available sunlight is trapped by photosynthesis. Tap each numbered arrow to see where the rest goes.

Pick a number to see the reason.
Exam tipLearn the four reasons as a list. "Explain why so little light energy is used" questions want these.

GPP and NPP

Class notes
Gross primary production (GPP)
Chemical energy store in plant biomass in a given area or volume. (This is the energy captured by photosynthesis.)
Net primary production (NPP)
The chemical energy store in plant biomass after respiratory losses to the environment.
NPP = GPP − R

R is respiratory losses

Extra help · why subtract R?Picture a bucket being filled by a tap (photosynthesis = GPP) with a leak in the side (the plant's own respiration = R). The plant needs to respire to stay alive, and that energy ends up as heat. What stays in the bucket is NPP.

This NPP is available for:

  1. Plant growth
  2. Plant reproduction
  3. Other trophic levels, e.g. herbivores or decomposers

Handwritten note NPP (biomass) may be passed on to the next (trophic) level, i.e. the primary consumer. There are large energy losses in this transfer.

The light energy which is converted into chemical energy in the form of organic molecules (glucose / cellulose / starch / amino acids / DNA etc.) is called:

Gross primary production (GPP).

Why: it is the total chemical energy stored by photosynthesis, before the plant has used any of it for respiration.

Your notes have "photosynthesis" written here. That is the process. The question asks for the name of the energy, which is GPP.

Original question

This NPP is available for: a) … b) … c) …

a) Plant growth · b) Plant reproduction · c) Other trophic levels, e.g. herbivores or decomposers.

Why: NPP is the energy left over after respiration, so it is the only energy that can build new biomass. Animals and decomposers can only get energy from that new biomass.

Section B · about 5 minutes

Why so much energy is lost along a food chain

Energy flow through a food chain

Class notes · Figure 1

Step through the chain. The percentages show roughly how much energy passes to the next level.

Scroll the diagram sideways to see all of it.

These percentages are only rough averages. They vary a lot between plants, animals and habitats.

Where the energy goes at each step

Class notes
  1. Large amounts of the plant may be indigestible and lost from the animal as faeces.
  2. Not all of the plant may be eaten (roots, bark etc.).
  3. Some energy is lost in excretion, such as energy lost in urine (consumer).
  4. Energy lost in respiration and as heat to the environment.
  1. Large amounts of the animal may be indigestible. Some of the net production therefore passes out in faeces.
  2. Not all of the animal may be eaten (bones and organs).
  3. Some energy is lost in excretion, such as energy lost in urine (consumer).
  4. Energy lost in respiration and as heat to the environment.

Energy that is stored as new biomass is available to the next trophic level.

Extra help · spot the patternThe four reasons are the same at both steps. Only the examples change: roots and bark for plants, bones and organs for animals. Plants contain a lot of indigestible material, which is part of why the first step (5–10%) is less efficient than later ones (15–20%).

Net production of consumers

Class notes
N = I − (F + R)
  • N is the net production
  • I is the chemical energy store of ingested food
  • F is the energy lost in faeces and urine
  • R is the energy lost in respiration
Extra help · in plain wordsTake what the animal eats. Subtract what comes out the other end (faeces and urine) and what is burned in respiration. What's left is new animal tissue, the only part the next level can eat.
Common mistakeIn this formula F covers faeces and urine. Some exam questions split urine out as a separate letter (the cow question uses U). Always read the key and subtract every loss.

So why are food chains short?

Class notes · summary
  • Energy transfer is inefficient and results in large losses at each trophic level.
  • Food chains are therefore usually no more than 4–5 trophic levels, because there is not enough energy to support a breeding population at a higher trophic level.

Section C · about 4 minutes

Calculating the efficiency of energy transfer

Units: energy ÷ area ÷ time

Class notes
On land
kJ m−2 year−1
per square metre (area)
Aquatic ecosystems
kJ m−3 year−1
per cubic metre (volume of water)

Each unit has three parts: Energy (kJ) · Area or volume (m−2 or m−3) · Time (year−1).

Extra help · why m⁻³ in water?Organisms live throughout the depth of a lake or sea, not just on a surface. So we measure per cube of water, not per square of ground.
% efficiency = energy available after transfer ÷ energy available before transfer × 100

Worked example

Class notes

The energy flow for a food chain on land, in kJ m−2 year−1:

Find the percentage efficiency of energy transfer between primary and secondary consumers.

  1. Step 1: find "after". The energy that reaches the secondary consumers = 714 kJ m−2 year−1.
  2. Step 2: find "before". The energy that was available in the primary consumers = 4032 kJ m−2 year−1.
  3. Step 3: divide after by before. 714 ÷ 4032 = 0.1771
  4. Step 4: × 100. 0.1771 × 100 = 17.7% (this sits inside the 15–20% in Figure 1)
Common mistakePutting the numbers upside down (4032 ÷ 714 = 565%). An efficiency can never be over 100%. If you get one, flip the fraction.

Exam practice: the cow question

Original questionTotal 5 marks

The diagram shows the transfer of energy through a cow. The figures are in kJ × 106 year−1.

Redrawn as a flow so it's easier to read. Same numbers as the cow drawing in your notes.

Scroll the diagram sideways to see all of it.

Key: A = energy absorbed from the gut · C = energy consumed in food · F = energy lost in faeces · P = energy used in production of new tissue · R = energy lost by respiration · U = energy lost in urine

Hint (from the question)When making calculations involving energy transfer, always remember that energy cannot be created or destroyed. In this type of question, this means that the total amount of energy entering a box must equal the amount of energy in the box plus the amount leaving the box.
Original question(1)

(a)(i) Complete the following equation for the energy used in the production of new tissue. Use only the letters C, F, R and U.

P =

P = C − (F + R + U)

also accepted: P = C − F − R − U

Why: energy can't be created or destroyed, so everything eaten (C) either leaves the cow (F, R, U) or stays as new tissue (P). Take the losses away from the input and you're left with P.

Not allowed: using A. The question says use only C, F, R and U.

Original question(1)

(ii) Calculate the value of P.

P =kJ × 106 year−1
  1. Add up the losses: F + R + U = 57.06 + 30.51 + 0.03 = 87.60
  2. Take them from the input: 91.34 − 87.60 = 3.74 kJ × 106 year−1
  3. Check it with the hint's box rule: C − F = 91.34 − 57.06 = 34.28, which matches A. Then A − R − U = 34.28 − 30.51 − 0.03 = 3.74 ✔
Original question(2)

(b) It has been estimated that an area of 8100 m2 of grassland is needed to keep one cow. The productivity of grass is 21 135 kJ m−2 year−1. What percentage of the energy in the grass is used in the production of new tissue in one cow? Show your working.

Answer%
  1. Energy in the grass. Productivity is per m², so multiply by the area: 21 135 × 8100 = 171 193 500 kJ year−1.
  2. Match the units. P is in kJ × 106, so write the grass energy the same way: 171 193 500 = 171.19 × 106 kJ year−1. (Or turn P into 3 740 000 kJ. Either works.)
  3. Percentage. after ÷ before × 100 = 3.74 ÷ 171.19 × 100 = 2.18% (2.2% is also fine).

Marks: 1 for correct working (finding the grass energy and dividing), 1 for the correct answer.

Why it's so small: most of the grass's energy is never eaten, is lost in faeces, or is used up in respiration.

Original question(1)

(c) Keeping cattle indoors, in barns, leads to a higher efficiency of energy transfer. Explain why.

Model answer: Less energy is lost as heat, and the cattle move around less, so less energy is used in respiration. More energy is therefore available for growth (P).

Why: in a warm barn the cow uses less energy keeping its body temperature up. In a small space it walks less. Both reduce R, and from P = C − (F + R + U), a smaller R means a bigger P.

For the mark, link the conditions (warmth or less movement) to less respiration or less heat loss.

Exam practice: productivity

Original question[2 marks]

Explain why an increase in shoot biomass can be taken as a measurement of net primary productivity.

  1. Biomass is a store of chemical energy (organic molecules made from photosynthesis). ✔
  2. New biomass is only made from the energy left after respiratory losses (NPP = GPP − R), so the increase in biomass shows net, not gross, production. ✔

Why: the plant respires all the time and that energy is lost as heat. It never shows up as biomass. So the biomass you can measure is what's left after respiration, which is the definition of NPP.

Nearly there. The summary is next.

Wrap-up · about 4 minutes

Summary

  • Only 1–3% of sunlight is captured: reflected, absorbed by atmosphere, wrong wavelength, misses chlorophyll.
  • GPP = chemical energy store in plant biomass in a given area or volume. NPP = GPP − R.
  • NPP is available for growth, reproduction and other trophic levels.
  • Consumers: N = I − (F + R). Losses: indigestible (faeces), not all eaten, urine, respiration and heat.
  • Big losses at every level → chains of 4–5 levels at most.
  • % efficiency = after ÷ before × 100. Units: kJ m−2 year−1 (land), kJ m−3 year−1 (water).
