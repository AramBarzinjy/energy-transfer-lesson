Section A · about 5 minutes

Why nutrients are recycled

The basic nutrient cycle

Class notes

Chemical elements are recycled in ecosystems, and microorganisms play a key role in this recycling. Recycling of chemical elements is important to maintain the balance of elements in the ecosystem.

Scroll the diagram sideways to see all of it.

Extra help · why it mattersElements like nitrogen and phosphorus aren't made fresh. The same atoms are used again and again. Without decomposers they would stay locked in dead bodies and waste, and plants would run out.

Saprobionts: how decomposers feed

Class notes

Decomposers are saprobiotic bacteria and fungi, also called saprobionts. Their way of feeding is called saprobiotic nutrition.

  1. Secrete enzymes. They secrete extracellular enzymes onto the dead matter through exocytosis.
  2. Digest outside the cell. The enzymes hydrolyse nitrogen-containing polymers (proteins, DNA, RNA) into monomers.
  3. Absorb. The monomers are absorbed into the saprobiont (through co-transport).
  4. Release ammonia. Ammonia (NH₃) is released into the soil. This process is called ammonification.
Extra help · in plain wordsSaprobionts can't swallow food. They pour enzymes out onto it, let it dissolve into small pieces, then soak the pieces up. "Extracellular" just means outside the cell.

Section B · about 4 minutes

Nitrogen in living things

Where nitrogen is found

Class notes

Nitrogen is found in molecules such as:

Handwritten note ATP: nitrogenous base. ATP contains nitrogen because its base, adenine, is a nitrogenous base.

Extra help · quick testCarbohydrates (glucose, starch) and triglycerides contain only C, H and O, so they have no nitrogen. If a molecule has a base (DNA, RNA, ATP) or amino groups (proteins), it contains nitrogen.

How nitrogen gets into plants

Class notes

Most organisms cannot use atmospheric nitrogen gas (N₂) directly, because it is chemically inert (unreactive). Most plants can only absorb nitrogen as nitrates (NO₃⁻) from the soil.

  1. Plants take up nitrogen from the soil as nitrate ions (NO₃⁻), absorbed by active transport through the root hair cells. This is one of the only routes for nitrogen to enter the living part of an ecosystem.
  2. Plants use the nitrogen to make amino acids and proteins, and other nitrogen-containing compounds like DNA and RNA. This allows the plants to grow and reproduce.
  3. Consumers eat the plants and hydrolyse proteins into amino acids. These are absorbed and assimilated into new tissue in the consumer.
  4. Primary consumers may be eaten by secondary consumers, so nitrogen passes up through the trophic levels.
  5. Both plants and animals die and get decomposed by saprobionts (Section A).
  6. Animals also excrete nitrogenous waste (urea).
Extra help · why active transport?There are usually more nitrate ions inside root hair cells than in the soil, so nitrate has to be pumped in against its concentration gradient. That uses ATP.

Details that earn marks

Class notes
  • Denitrification only occurs in waterlogged, anaerobic conditions. The notes add that it is a natural way to prevent leaching.
  • Nitrogen fixation: nitrogen-fixing bacteria live free in the soil and in the root nodules of some leguminous plants. They reduce N₂ gas to ammonium ions or compounds, which plants can then use.
  • Lightning: electrical fixation of N₂ gas in the atmosphere into nitrates in the soil.
Extra help · ammonia or ammonium?Ammonia (NH₃) dissolves in soil water to form ammonium ions (NH₄⁺). The notes use both names for the same stage. Either is accepted in exam answers.
Watch out · a slip in the notesPoint 11 says "nitrogen fixing bacteria and fungi". Only bacteria fix nitrogen. Fungi are decomposers here. Write "nitrogen-fixing bacteria" in the exam.
Memory hookFixation gives ammonia. Nitrification goes NH₃ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ (adds oxygen = oxidation). Denitrification destroys nitrate, back to N₂, and only without oxygen.

Section D · about 5 minutes · exam question from your notes

Exam practice

Original question(5)

Describe how the action of microorganisms in the soil produces a source of nitrates for crop plants. (5)

Mark scheme (from your notes). Any 5 of these 7 points:

  1. Protein / amino acids / DNA converted into ammonium compounds / ammonia;
  2. By saprobionts;
  3. Ammonium / ammonia into nitrite;
  4. (then) Nitrite into nitrate;
  5. By nitrifying bacteria / microorganisms;
  6. Nitrogen gas to ammonia / ammonium;
  7. By nitrogen-fixing bacteria / microorganisms in soil;

How to build the answer, step by step

  1. Start with dead matter. Name the molecule (protein, DNA), what it becomes (ammonia) and who does it (saprobionts). That's 2 marks.
  2. Do nitrification in two steps. Ammonia → nitrite, then nitrite → nitrate, by nitrifying bacteria. That's 3 more marks. Writing it as two separate steps is what earns points 3 and 4.
  3. Add the second source. Nitrogen gas → ammonia by nitrogen-fixing bacteria. That's a backup 2 marks in case you missed any above.

Model answer: Saprobionts break down proteins, amino acids and DNA in dead organisms and waste into ammonia / ammonium compounds (ammonification). Nitrifying bacteria then oxidise ammonium into nitrite, and then nitrite into nitrate (nitrification). Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the soil also convert nitrogen gas into ammonia / ammonium, which can then be nitrified into nitrate.

Don't includeDenitrification removes nitrate, so it can't be a source of nitrate. Lightning isn't a microorganism, so it earns nothing here. You don't need species names.

Wrap-up · about 4 minutes

Summary

  • Elements are recycled to keep their balance in the ecosystem. Microorganisms play the key role.
  • Saprobionts secrete enzymes (exocytosis), hydrolyse polymers, absorb monomers and release ammonia: ammonification.
  • Nitrogen is in amino acids, proteins, DNA, RNA, ATP, ADP, NAD, NADP and FAD.
  • N₂ is inert. Plants absorb nitrate by active transport through root hair cells.
  • Nitrification (nitrifying bacteria): NH₃ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻, oxidation.
  • Nitrogen fixation (nitrogen-fixing bacteria, free in soil or in legume root nodules): N₂ → NH₃, reduction.
  • Denitrification (denitrifying bacteria): NO₃⁻ → N₂, only in waterlogged, anaerobic soil.
