Selection and speciation

This topic has lots of words, but it tells one story: living things vary, the environment picks which ones survive, allele frequencies change, and given enough time and isolation you get new species. Each section is one chapter of that story, with models to play with. Stop whenever you like: your place is saved.

Variation → selection pressure → differential survival and reproduction → allele frequency changes → (with isolation) new species

Section A · about 5 minutes · chapter 1 of the story

Variation: the raw material

Why individuals differ

Class notes · p3

Individuals in a population show a wide range of variation in phenotype (what they look like and how they work). This is due to genetic and environmental factors.

Three causes of genetic variation (in sexual reproduction):

  1. Mutation: the primary source. A sudden change in the base sequence of DNA, which introduces new alleles.
  2. Meiosis: crossing over and independent segregation shuffle alleles into new combinations.
  3. Random fertilisation: any gamete can fuse with any other.
Memory trick · M M R

Mutation makes new alleles. Meiosis mixes them. Random fertilisation combines them.

A mutation could give a selective advantage, be neutral, or give a selective disadvantage.

The environment also affects phenotype: genotype sets the limits; the environment decides where within those limits the organism ends up. Environmental factors include temperature, light intensity, humidity and other abiotic and biotic factors.

Two kinds of variation

Class notes · p3–4
Discontinuous
A few distinct categories, no in-betweens. Mainly genetic, usually one gene. Plot as a bar chart. E.g. blood group.
Continuous
Any value in a range. Controlled by many genes (polygenes) and the environment. Plot as a normal distribution (bell curve). E.g. height, mass.
Study tip (from the notes)Recognise discontinuous variation and be able to plot a bar chart from any data you're given. Continuous data gets a histogram or line, never separated bars.

Section B · about 6 minutes · chapter 2

Natural selection

The definition

Class notes · p4
Natural selection
The process by which organisms that are better adapted to their environment survive and reproduce in greater numbers, resulting in an increase in the frequency of the advantageous allele within the population.
Evolution
A change in the allele frequencies within a population (over many generations).

The chain, one link at a time

Class notes · p4–5
  1. Over-production. Organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support.
  2. Variation exists within the population, caused by mutation (plus meiosis and random fertilisation).
  3. Selection pressure: predation, disease or competition for limited resources (intraspecific competition). These can be biotic (living) or abiotic (non-living).
  4. Differential survival: individuals with an advantageous allele are more likely to survive and reproduce. Less well adapted ones are less likely to.
  5. They pass on their advantageous alleles to their offspring.
  6. Over many generations the frequency of the advantageous allele increases in the population.
Exam trick · the 4-mark skeleton

Variation (mutation) → selection pressure → better adapted survive and reproduce → pass on allele → allele frequency increases. Almost every natural selection question is this chain with the context's words dropped in.

The classic mistakeOrganisms do not develop a phenotype because the environment changes. The useful allele was already there, from a chance mutation, before the change. Never write “the moths turned dark to hide”.

Play it: be the bird

Extra help · model

Extra help · model

Pick a type, then press Next generation a few times. The red shading shows the phenotypes being selected against.

The details from the notes

Class notes · p6–8

Stabilising: occurs where the environment is stable. Selection pressure at both ends; favours the average; reduces variability; reduces the opportunity for evolutionary change. E.g. very heavy or very light babies have higher death rates around birth, so over time there are fewer of them.

Directional: the mean was the optimum, but an environmental change brings a new selection pressure that favours one extreme. Some individuals already have that phenotype (from a chance mutation); over time it predominates and the mean shifts. E.g. mice: at 10 °C the best fur length is 1.5 cm; if it cools to 5 °C, mice with fur ≥ 2.0 cm survive better, and the mean shifts to 2.0 cm. Then the pressure stops.

Disruptive: the opposite of stabilising. Favours two extremes; the least common type, but the most important for speciation, because it can split one population into two. E.g. light mice on sand and dark mice in grass; medium mice get eaten.

The notes say the mean “shifts in both directions” in disruptive selection. Better to say the population splits into two peaks: the overall mean can stay where it was.

Section D · about 7 minutes · the maths bit

Gene pools and Hardy–Weinberg

The words

Class notes + AQA 3.7.2
Population
All the organisms of one species living in the same place at the same time, which can interbreed.
Gene pool
All the alleles of all the genes in a population at one time.
Allele frequency
How often an allele occurs in the gene pool, as a proportion (0 to 1).

The two equations

AQA 3.7.2

For a gene with two alleles, dominant A (frequency p) and recessive a (frequency q):

p + q = 1   (alleles)
p² + 2pq + q² = 1   (genotypes)
TermGenotypePhenotype
p²AA homozygous dominantdominant
2pqAa heterozygous (carriers)dominant
q²aa homozygous recessiverecessive: the only one you can see directly
The golden rule

Always start from q². The recessive phenotype is the only genotype you can count, because AA and Aa look the same. q² → √ → q → p = 1 − q → then p² and 2pq.

When does it work? The 5 conditions

Class notes · p10 (filled in)

Hardy–Weinberg predicts allele frequencies will stay constant from one generation to the next, as long as:

  1. The population is large.
  2. Mating is random.
  3. There is no mutation.
  4. There is no migration in or out (no gene flow).
  5. There is no selection (all genotypes equally likely to survive and reproduce).
Memory trick · “Large Random Mutant-free Mice, Never Selected”

Large · Random mating · no Mutation · no Migration · no Selection.

The point: if real allele frequencies do change, one of these conditions is being broken, often selection. That's how Hardy–Weinberg shows evolution happening.

Original question · textbook Q5(a)[3]

A gene associated with copper tolerance has two alleles, T and t. Varieties of Agrostis tenuis that have both recessive alleles are copper tolerant, while varieties possessing one or more dominant alleles are not. A sample of plants was tested for copper tolerance and 72 plants were found to be copper tolerant while 378 were not.
(a) Assuming the Hardy–Weinberg principle applies, calculate the percentage of the sample that have the genotypes TT, Tt and tt.

%
%
%
  1. Total = 72 + 378 = 450. Tolerant plants are tt, so q² = 72 ÷ 450 = 0.16 → 16%
  2. q = √0.16 = 0.4; p = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6
  3. TT = p² = 0.6² = 0.36 = 36%
  4. Tt = 2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.48 = 48%
  5. Check: 36 + 48 + 16 = 100 ✓
Original question · textbook Q5(b)[2]

(b) Justify, giving two reasons, why the Hardy–Weinberg principle does not apply in the situation described here.

Any two:

  1. Selection is happening: copper in the soil favours the tolerant (tt) plants. ✔
  2. Mating may not be random: plants on contaminated soil are more likely to pollinate each other. ✔
  3. Migration / gene flow: pollen and seeds can blow in from plants on uncontaminated soil. ✔
  4. The sample / population may be small. ✔

Section E · about 4 minutes

Genetic drift: evolution by chance

What it is

Class notes · p11, tidied
Genetic drift
A random (chance) change in allele frequency from one generation to the next. It matters most in small populations, where the chance loss of a few individuals carrying an allele changes its frequency a lot.
Bottleneck (a type of drift)
A large population is suddenly cut to a few survivors by chance, e.g. a natural disaster: volcanic eruption, tsunami, meteor. The survivors' alleles form the new gene pool, so genetic diversity falls.
Founder effect (a type of drift)
A small group leaves to found a new population, e.g. on an island. It carries only some of the alleles of the original population, by chance.
Gene flow (not drift)
Change in allele frequency due to the migration of fertile individuals between populations.
Correction to the notes (p11)The table puts “founder effect” in the same row as gene flow (migration). They're different: gene flow is migration between populations; the founder effect is genetic drift in a small new population.
Selection vs drift

Selection: which individuals survive depends on their alleles. Drift: survival is luck. A volcano doesn't care about your fur length.

Section F · about 7 minutes · the last chapter

Speciation: making new species

The key idea

Class notes · p10
Species
A group of similar organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring. They share one gene pool.
Speciation
The evolution of a new species from an existing species.

If two populations can still interbreed, they won't become separate species: interbreeding keeps mixing their gene pools. So first they must be reproductively isolated (prevented from interbreeding). Then mutations accumulate and different selection pressures act on each, until they're too different to produce fertile offspring.

No isolation → no speciation. Isolation is always step one.

Allopatric speciation, step by step

Class notes · p11

Allopatric = “different countries” = geographical separation. Darwin's finches are the classic example: one mainland ancestor reached the separate Galápagos islands and became many species with different beaks for different foods (seeds, insects, cactus, buds).

Sympatric speciation: same place

Class notes · p12

Sympatric = “same country”. There is no geographical barrier; the populations live in the same area but become reproductively separated another way, e.g. choice of food, mating season, or becoming active at a different time after hibernation.

Apple maggot fly
Flies mate only on their host plant's fruit. Flies that feed on hawthorn don't meet flies that feed on apples, so the two groups are genetically isolated and are becoming different species.
Lord Howe Island palms
Two palm species on one island, with different flowering times, so they can't pollinate each other.
Memory trick

Allopatric: all over the place (separated). Sympatric: the same place.

Isolating mechanisms: you need to know these

Section G · exam questions from the notes

Exam practice

How these are marked

Each “;” in a mark scheme is one marking point. Speciation answers almost always need: isolation → no interbreeding → variation by mutation → different selection pressures → survive and reproduce → allele frequency changes → over a long time → can't produce fertile offspring.

Original question(6)

Changes in ecosystems can lead to speciation. In Southern California 10 000 years ago a number of interconnecting lakes contained a single species of pupfish. Increasing temperatures caused evaporation and the formation of separate, smaller lakes and streams. This led to the formation of a number of different species of pupfish.
Explain how these different species evolved.

Mark scheme (from the notes), max 6:

  1. Geographical isolation;
  2. Separate gene pools / no interbreeding (between populations);
  3. Variation due to mutation;
  4. Different environmental / abiotic / biotic conditions / selection pressures;
  5. Selection for different / advantageous features / characteristics / mutation / allele;
  6. Differential reproductive success / (selected) organisms survive and reproduce;
  7. Leads to change in allele frequency;
  8. Occurs over a long period of time;
Original question(6)

Changes in ecosystems can lead to speciation. A high concentration of copper in soil is toxic to most plants. In some areas where the soil is polluted with copper, populations of grasses are found to be growing. These populations of grass belong to a species also found growing on unpolluted soils. It has been suggested that a new species of grass may evolve on soil that has been polluted with copper.
Explain how this new species might evolve.

Mark scheme (from the notes):

  1. Variation / variety;
  2. (caused by) Mutation;
  3. Some plants have allele to survive / grow / live in high concentration of copper / polluted soils;
  4. (Differential) reproductive success / adapted organisms reproduce;
  5. Increase in frequency of advantageous allele;
  6. No interbreeding (with other populations) / separate gene pool / gene pool differs (from other populations);
Original question · model answer in notes(6)

Lord Howe Island in the Tasman Sea possesses two species of palm tree which have arisen via sympatric speciation. The two species diverged from each other after the island was formed 6.5 million years ago. The flowering times of the two species are different.
Using this information, suggest how these two species of palm tree arose by sympatric speciation.

  1. Occurs in the same habitat / environment / population; (accept: not geographically isolated)
  2. Mutation/s cause different flowering times;
  3. Reproductive separation / isolation OR no gene flow OR gene pools remain separate;
  4. Different allele/s passed on / selected OR change in frequency of allele/s;
  5. Disruptive (natural) selection;
  6. Eventually different species cannot (inter)breed to produce fertile offspring;

Notice point 5: sympatric speciation usually involves disruptive selection. That's why the notes call it the most important type for evolution.

Original questions 1–4
  1. Identify the types of curve shown in Figure 2.
  2. Name the type of inheritance these curves suggest.
  3. Suggest why the population of copper-tolerant varieties where the soil is heavily contaminated with copper is much larger than the population of non-tolerant varieties where the soil is less contaminated.

Wrap-up · about 4 minutes

The whole story

  1. Variation: mutation (new alleles), meiosis, random fertilisation, plus environment. Discontinuous (one gene, categories) vs continuous (polygenes + environment, normal curve).
  2. Natural selection: selection pressure → better adapted survive and reproduce → pass on alleles → allele frequency increases. Evolution = change in allele frequency.
  3. Types: stabilising (squeezes), directional (shifts), disruptive (splits).
  4. Hardy–Weinberg: p + q = 1; p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Start from q². Needs large, random mating, no mutation, migration or selection.
  5. Genetic drift: chance changes, biggest in small populations (bottleneck, founder effect).
  6. Speciation: reproductive isolation → separate gene pools → different mutations and selection → can't produce fertile offspring. Allopatric (geographical) or sympatric (same place).
