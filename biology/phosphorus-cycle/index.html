Section A · about 5 minutes

The phosphorus cycle

Why phosphorus matters

Class notes

Phosphorus is a component of ATP, phospholipids and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). Life depends on it being constantly recycled.

  • In the phosphorus cycle there are no gases involved. (Unlike nitrogen, there's no "phosphorus in the air" stage.)
  • Phosphorus exists mostly as phosphate ions (PO₄³⁻) in sedimentary rock.
  • Weathering and erosion of the rocks help phosphate ions dissolve in water in lakes and rivers. Plants absorb them and build them into their biomass.
  • Saprobiotic bacteria decompose the phosphorus compounds in excreta and dead organisms.

Two definitions

Class notes
Erosion
Once rock has been weakened and broken up by weathering, it is ready for erosion. Erosion happens when rocks and sediments are picked up and moved to another place by ice, water, wind or gravity. There can also be chemical erosion.
Deposition
The geological process in which sediments, soil and rocks are added to a landform or land mass. Previously eroded sediment is deposited when the fluid carrying it loses enough kinetic energy, building up layers of sediment.

Section B · about 3 minutes

The role of mycorrhizae

What they are

Class notes

Mycorrhizae are associations between specific types of fungi and the root systems of plants. The fungus grows a fine network of threads through the soil around the roots.

Mycorrhizae benefit plants by:

  1. increasing the total surface area of the plant's roots
  2. increasing the absorption of water
  3. increasing the absorption of mineral ions, for example phosphates and nitrates

The notes' pictures show this: a tomato plant with mycorrhizae has a much wider root network, and grows bigger with more fruit, than one without.

A mutualistic relationship

Class notes

The relationship is mutualistic: both benefit.

The plant gets
more water and mineral ions (phosphates, nitrates), through a bigger absorbing surface area
The fungus gets
organic molecules from the plant, e.g. sucrose and amino acids
Extra help · why the fungus needs thisFungi can't photosynthesise. The plant makes sugars in its leaves, and passing some to the fungus is the "payment" for the extra water and ions.

Section C · about 5 minutes

Fertilisers and their impact

Why fertilisers are needed

Class notes

Plants need light and CO₂ to photosynthesise, and inorganic ions to make compounds such as proteins. In a natural ecosystem the ions are recycled. In agricultural ecosystems the crops are harvested and removed, so there is less recycling.

  1. Crops are harvested and removed, so they don't die and decompose in the field.
  2. So fewer inorganic ions are returned to the soil.
  3. Nitrogen is needed to make proteins and DNA, both needed for growth. With fewer ions, plants grow more slowly.
  4. Slower growth means a smaller leaf area, so less photosynthesis and lower productivity.
  5. So the lost ions must be replaced using fertilisers.
Examiner tip (from the notes)Fertilisers commonly contain nitrates, phosphates and potassium ions. You may be asked why plants need them, with examples of molecules containing them. Phosphorus is in DNA, RNA, ATP and the phospholipids that form all cell membranes.

Two types of fertiliser

Class notes
Natural
Waste materials such as manure.
Artificial
Manufactured, man-made powders or pellets made in factories, containing nitrates, phosphates and potassium ions.

The environmental impact

Class notes
Reduced species diversity
Plants adapted to soils high in nitrate outcompete other species.
Leaching
Artificial fertilisers put more ions onto the soil than the plants take up. The ions dissolve in soil water and are washed out of the soil into streams, rivers and lakes.

The notes also repeat the main reason fertilisers are used: in agricultural ecosystems crops are removed and there's less decomposition, so less nitrate returns to the soil. Fertilisers replace the nitrates and phosphorus.

Section D · about 5 minutes

Exam practice

Original question(5)

Describe how phosphorus in dead organisms is recycled so that it becomes available to plants again. (5)

The notes show only this question's mark scheme. The question wording here is reconstructed to match it.

Mark scheme (from the notes), 5 max:

  1. Phospholipids, RNA and DNA from dead organisms into phosphate ions;
  2. Decomposed by saprobionts;
  3. Phosphate ions also found in waste and remains, e.g. guano, bones and shells;
  4. Phosphate ions transported by streams, rivers, lakes and oceans;
  5. Form sedimentary rock;
  6. Weathering and erosion of rocks helps phosphate ions dissolve in oceans, lakes and soils;
  7. Phosphates become available for absorption by plants to incorporate into biomass;

How to build it: follow the cycle in order. Molecules → saprobionts → phosphate ions → carried by water → rock → weathering and erosion → dissolved → absorbed by plants. Each arrow is a mark.

Model answer: Saprobionts decompose phospholipids, DNA and RNA in dead organisms, releasing phosphate ions. Phosphate is also found in wastes and remains such as guano, bones and shells. Phosphate ions are carried by streams and rivers into lakes and oceans, where they form sedimentary rock. Weathering and erosion of the rock help phosphate ions dissolve in oceans, lakes and soils, where they are absorbed by plants and incorporated into their biomass.

Wrap-up · about 3 minutes

Summary

  • Phosphorus is in ATP, phospholipids and nucleic acids. The cycle has no gases.
  • Phosphate is stored in sedimentary rock. Weathering and erosion dissolve it into water; plants absorb it; animals get it by feeding.
  • Saprobiotic bacteria decompose excreta and dead organisms. Remains (guano, bones, shells) are eroded or deposited to form rock again.
  • Mycorrhizae: fungi + roots. Bigger surface area, more water and mineral ions. Mutualistic: the fungus gets sucrose and amino acids.
  • Fertilisers replace ions lost when crops are harvested. Natural (manure) or artificial (nitrates, phosphates, potassium).
  • Impact: reduced species diversity, and leaching of excess ions into rivers and lakes.
